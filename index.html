Welcome · Why is AI important to all of us?

Responsible AI begins with informed people

Artificial Intelligence is a transformative force in higher education. It is changing how we teach, how we learn, how we conduct research and how we work.

New tools are emerging almost daily, creating exciting opportunities alongside important questions about ethics, integrity, trust and professional practice. At SETU, we believe that AI is not simply another technology to adopt. It is something that challenges us to think differently about knowledge, expertise and the role of the human in an increasingly AI-enabled world.

This learning journey has been designed to help you develop the confidence, understanding and professional judgement needed to use AI responsibly and effectively in your own role. Whether you are an academic, researcher or member of our Professional, Management and Support Services, the aim is not to turn you into an AI expert. Instead, it is to help you become an informed, critical and confident professional who knows when AI can add value, when caution is needed and when human expertise matters most.

Understanding AI · What exactly is AI?

Understanding the language of AI

Before we explore how AI can support teaching, research and professional practice, it helps to understand some of the terms you'll hear most often.

~7 min Includes an activity

Words like Artificial Intelligence, Machine Learning, Large Language Models and Generative AI are often used interchangeably in the media, but they don't all mean the same thing. Understanding the differences doesn't require technical expertise — it simply helps us make sense of the rapidly changing AI landscape. Let's start with the biggest picture.

Artificial IntelligenceThe broad term for computer systems that can perform tasks that normally require human intelligence — recognising patterns, understanding language, making predictions, solving problems and supporting decisions. Not a single technology, but an umbrella. Think of AI as the largest circle; inside it are several more specialised technologies.
Machine LearningInstead of being programmed with every possible rule, machine-learning systems learn from data. By analysing thousands — or millions — of examples, they identify patterns that let them make predictions or classifications. Spam filtering and Netflix recommendations often use machine learning behind the scenes.
Large Language ModelsA specialised type of machine-learning model trained on enormous collections of text. Its purpose is to predict the most likely sequence of words based on the prompt it receives. LLMs can answer questions, summarise information, explain concepts, draft emails and generate code — and they power many of today's most popular AI assistants.
Generative AIAI systems that create new content — text, images, presentations, audio, video or code. Asking ChatGPT to draft an email, Copilot to summarise a meeting, or an image generator to create an illustration all use Generative AI. Generative AI describes what the system does; Large Language Models describe the technology behind many of these tools.
AI AgentsThe newest development. Unlike a chatbot answering a single question, an agent can work towards a goal across multiple connected tasks — gathering information, using different tools, making decisions along the way and adapting as new information appears. As these systems become more capable, human judgement becomes more important, not less: people remain responsible for setting goals, reviewing outputs and making final decisions.
Activity Which of these use AI?

Select all the ones you think use AI, then reveal the answers.

Most people associate AI with ChatGPT, but AI has been quietly supporting many everyday digital services. Recommendation systems, navigation apps, facial recognition and spam filters all rely on AI techniques. By contrast, a standard calculator or washing machine follows predefined rules rather than making intelligent decisions based on data.

Key messageBringing it all together

  • Artificial Intelligence is the broad field.
  • Machine Learning is one approach within it.
  • Large Language Models are a type of machine-learning model designed to work with language.
  • Generative AI uses technologies such as LLMs to create new content.
  • AI Agents build on these capabilities to complete more complex, multi-step tasks.

You don't need to remember every definition — just recognise that these terms describe different parts of the same AI ecosystem, each with different capabilities and limitations.

How AI Works · Why does AI behave the way it does?

How does Generative AI work?

You don't need to understand the mathematics behind AI to use it well. But a basic sense of how it works helps you write better prompts, recognise its limits, and decide when to trust — or question — its responses.

~9 min

Prediction, not knowledge

One of the biggest misconceptions about AI is that it "knows" things. It doesn't. Large Language Models don't think like humans, search the internet every time you ask, or retrieve facts from a hidden database. Instead, they work by predicting what text is most likely to come next.

Imagine someone starts a sentence: "The capital of France is…" Most people would instinctively say "Paris" — not because they calculated the answer, but because they have seen that sequence of words many times before. Generative AI works in a similar way, but on a vastly larger scale. It has learned patterns from enormous amounts of text and uses them to predict the next word, then the next, until it produces a complete response.

Watch it build a sentence one prediction at a time:

Try itComplete the sentence

What word comes next? Say each one in your head, then reveal.

  • Roses are red…
  • The early bird catches the…
  • Once upon a…

You've just done what an LLM does — predict the most likely next word, based on patterns you've seen many times before.

Probability

Every time the AI generates a word, it is making a prediction based on probability. Rather than deciding one word is "correct", it considers many possible next words and estimates which is most likely to fit the context. The most probable choice is usually selected — though some tools introduce a little randomness to make responses more creative or varied. That's why you may get slightly different answers when asking the same question more than once.

Tokens

Although we think in words and sentences, AI works with much smaller pieces of language called tokens. A token might be a whole word, part of a word, a punctuation mark or even a space. When you write a prompt, the AI breaks it into tokens, processes them, and predicts the next token one at a time. You don't need to count tokens yourself, but understanding this helps explain why very long conversations eventually reach a limit.

Context windows

Every AI model has a limit to how much information it can keep "in mind" during a conversation. This is known as its context window, and it includes:

  • your prompt
  • previous messages in the conversation
  • documents you've uploaded
  • the AI's earlier responses

If a conversation becomes very long, older details may be forgotten or summarised to make room for new content. That's why it's sometimes helpful to start a fresh conversation or restate important information.

Hallucinations

Sometimes AI produces information that sounds convincing but is incorrect. This is known as a hallucination, and it can include invented references, fabricated quotations, incorrect statistics, imaginary policies, inaccurate summaries, and confident but false explanations.

Hallucinations don't happen because the AI is trying to mislead you. They happen because the model is always trying to generate the most probable continuation of text — even when it doesn't have reliable information. This is why human judgement remains essential. Always verify important facts before using AI-generated content.

When AI takes things literally

Some hallucinations are memorable. Real examples have included lawyers submitting court filings that cited cases which never existed, and image generators taking a phrase like “salmon swimming upstream” and cheerfully producing fillets of salmon bobbing along a river. They are a useful reminder: AI produces what is probable or literal, not necessarily what is true or intended.

Try it Spot the hallucination. Three AI answers respond to “Suggest a source on assessment design.” Which one should set off alarm bells?

The third answer is the risky one. A precise-looking citation — real-sounding authors, journal, page numbers and a DOI — is exactly what a fabricated reference looks like. Confidence and specificity are not evidence of accuracy. Always verify a reference exists in a trusted database before using it.

Reasoning

Recent AI models are much better at solving complex problems because they can spend more time working through a task before answering. This is sometimes described as reasoning: rather than immediately generating an answer, the model can analyse information, compare options and break a complicated task into smaller steps.

It's important to remember that reasoning is not the same as human understanding. Even highly capable models can make mistakes, overlook context or reach incorrect conclusions. The role of the human is still to evaluate the output, apply professional expertise and make the final decision.

Context matters

A quick illustration of why the same tool gives very different results depending on what you tell it:

Prompt 1 — generic result

“Write an email.”

Prompt 2 — much better

“Write a polite email to first-year nursing students reminding them to submit their placement documentation by Friday. The tone should be supportive rather than formal.”

Key messageThe bottom line

  • Generative AI doesn't think, understand or know things the way people do.
  • It predicts patterns in language remarkably well, which lets it produce useful, creative and often impressive responses.
  • It can hallucinate — sound confident while being wrong — so always verify important facts.
  • The better we understand its strengths and limitations, the better equipped we are to use it responsibly.

The next question is the most important one: when should we use it, and when shouldn't we?

Where AI needs you

Despite its impressive capabilities, AI has important limitations. Understanding them is one of the most valuable skills you can develop.

AI can be biased

Models learn from human-created data. If that data contains historical bias or under-represents groups, that bias can appear in outputs. Never use AI uncritically for decisions that affect people.

AI can hallucinate

It sometimes generates invented facts, fabricated quotations or fictional policies — presented with complete confidence. Always verify before relying on it.

It may fabricate references

One of the most common mistakes: creating references that look genuine but don't exist. Never assume AI-generated citations are accurate without checking the sources.

Knowledge may be outdated

Some tools are trained only up to a certain point in time; others have live access. Either way, don't assume complete or current knowledge of fast-changing topics like legislation or policy.

It sounds more confident than it should

AI is designed to produce fluent, coherent language, so an incorrect answer can sound just as convincing as a correct one. Confidence should never be mistaken for accuracy.

It doesn't know your context

AI doesn't automatically understand your students, discipline or institutional policies. Better context leads to better outputs — but ensuring that context is accurate remains your responsibility.

Privacy matters

Information entered into AI tools may be processed outside the university. Before using AI, consider whether the data is personal, confidential or commercially sensitive, and follow institutional guidance.

Copyright and IP

The relationship between AI and copyright is still evolving. Consider whether outputs incorporate copyrighted material, whether you may upload existing content, and how AI use should be acknowledged.

Environmental impact

Training and running large models takes significant energy. Individual prompts use little, but responsible AI means using these tools thoughtfully rather than unnecessarily.

  • Digital doesn't mean impact-free. Responsible AI includes considering whether the value of using AI justifies the resources involved.

What this means in practice

Human oversight

One of the most important principles in responsible AI is maintaining meaningful human oversight. AI can support decision-making; it should not replace it. Whether you're drafting a policy, preparing teaching materials, analysing information or supporting students, you remain responsible for reviewing outputs, checking accuracy and making the final decision. Accountability cannot be delegated to AI.

Transparency

Transparency helps to build trust. Where AI has made a significant contribution to a piece of work, consider whether that use should be acknowledged. Within teaching and assessment, students should also understand when AI use is permitted, expected or restricted. Being open about AI use supports honesty, consistency and shared expectations.

Academic integrity

Generative AI presents new opportunities for learning — but also new challenges. At SETU, academic integrity remains a fundamental value. Students should use AI in ways that align with module expectations and assessment guidance, and staff should use AI responsibly when designing assessments, developing materials and providing feedback. The goal is not to prohibit AI, but to ensure its use supports genuine learning and maintains confidence in our qualifications.

Critical thinking

Perhaps the most important skill in an AI-enabled world is critical thinking. AI can generate ideas, summarise information and suggest solutions. It cannot replace the human ability to evaluate evidence, consider context, exercise professional judgement and make ethical decisions. The most effective users of AI are not those who accept every response — they are those who ask good questions, challenge assumptions, and know when to rely on their own expertise.

Scenario A colleague is short on time and suggests uploading a batch of students’ submitted assignments — names and all — into a free public AI chatbot, so it can draft grades and feedback to save everyone time. What is the most responsible response?

The third option. Two principles collide here: privacy/data protection (student work and identities must not go into a public tool that may store or train on them) and human oversight (marking is an academic judgement that can’t be delegated to AI). Removing names isn’t enough — assignments can still identify students and may be confidential. AI can support the process (rubrics, feedback templates), but the decisions, and the data, stay with people and approved systems.

Key messageResponsibility stays with people

  • Responsible AI is about judgement, not just rules — keep people at the centre.
  • Protect personal and confidential data; use approved tools and follow SETU guidance.
  • Maintain human oversight, be transparent about AI use, and uphold academic integrity.
  • Accountability cannot be delegated to AI.

Working Effectively with AI · How do I collaborate with AI?

Working effectively with AI

Many people talk about “prompt engineering” as a specialist technical skill. In reality, the most effective AI users are those who communicate clearly, think critically and apply their own professional judgement.

~6 min Read

Working with AI is less about writing the perfect prompt and more about having an effective conversation. A simple five-step approach can help.

Think

Before opening an AI tool, pause and ask:

  • Is AI appropriate for this task?
  • Does the task involve personal, confidential or sensitive information?
  • Will AI genuinely add value?
  • What role should AI play — and what should remain my responsibility?

Choosing when not to use AI is just as important as knowing when to use it.

Ask

Good outputs begin with clear instructions. The more context you provide, the more useful the response. Explain what you're trying to achieve, who the audience is, the tone you want, any constraints, and the format you'd like.

Prompt 1

“Write an email.”

Prompt 2

“Draft a friendly email to first-year students reminding them to submit their placement documentation by Friday. Keep the tone supportive and under 200 words.”

Refine

Your first response is rarely your final response. Treat AI as a collaborator: ask follow-up questions, request alternatives, ask for more detail, simplify complex explanations, change the tone, and build on good ideas. Iterative conversations often produce much better results than expecting perfection first time.

Review

Never assume AI is correct. Before using AI-generated content, review it carefully:

  • Is it accurate? Is it complete?
  • Does it reflect SETU guidance and policies?
  • Is anything missing? Does it contain bias?
  • Are references genuine? Does it sound like me?

This step is where your expertise becomes most valuable.

Decide

The final decision always belongs to you. AI can suggest, draft, summarise and analyse — but AI cannot take responsibility. Whether you're teaching students, conducting research, supporting colleagues or making institutional decisions, you remain accountable for the quality and integrity of your work. The goal isn't to rely on AI. It's to use it wisely.

Try it Improve this prompt. You want AI to help you write to students about a room change. Which is the strongest prompt?

The third prompt. It gives the AI what it needs to be useful: audience (Tuesday 10am first-years), purpose and detail (the specific room change and when), tone (warm but clear) and format/length (a short email under 120 words). “Make it nice” sounds friendly but tells the AI almost nothing. Context is what turns a generic reply into a usable draft — then you review and decide.

Key messageJudgement makes AI valuable

  • The most effective users aren't those who know the most prompts.
  • They ask thoughtful questions, provide context, and review outputs critically.
  • They know when to rely on their own expertise instead.
  • AI is a powerful collaborator — your judgement is what makes it valuable.

AI in practice

AI can support many aspects of university life, but the way it is used varies by role. These examples show how colleagues across SETU might use AI responsibly — as a professional assistant, with human expertise at the centre.

  • generate discussion questions
  • develop case studies
  • create rubrics

  • suggest alternative explanations for difficult concepts

Assessment

AI can support assessment by helping staff to:

  • develop authentic assessment ideas
  • create marking criteria
  • draft feedback templates
  • review assessment wording for clarity
  • map assessments to learning outcomes

Assessment decisions, marking and academic judgement always remain the responsibility of the academic.

Case study — Sarah, Programme Leader

Sarah wants to redesign an assessment for a large first-year class. She uses AI to generate five authentic assessment ideas, compares them with the Assessment Redesign Framework, discusses the options with colleagues and adapts one to suit her module.

Where did AI add value? Where was Sarah's expertise essential?

AI added value by quickly generating a range of starting ideas Sarah could react to. Sarah's expertise was essential in judging which idea was academically sound, checking it against the Assessment Redesign Framework, discussing it with colleagues, and adapting it to her students and learning outcomes. The decision — and the accountability — stayed with her.

Research

Researchers may use AI to:

  • explore unfamiliar topics
  • generate research questions
  • summarise literature
  • identify themes in qualitative data
  • assist with coding
  • improve the readability of manuscripts

AI should never replace critical evaluation of evidence or verification of sources.

Professional, Management & Support Services

AI can assist colleagues across professional services by helping to:

  • draft emails and reports
  • summarise meetings
  • prepare presentations
  • analyse survey responses
  • develop project plans
  • improve policies and procedures
  • organise information
  • support communication with different audiences

Professional judgement remains essential when making decisions or communicating on behalf of the university.

Case study — David, Registry

David receives repeated queries about progression regulations. He asks AI to draft a plain-English explanation of a complex policy. He checks the wording against the official regulations before publishing it on the website.

What should David check before using the AI output?

David should check the AI's plain-English version against the official regulations for accuracy, make sure no nuance or exceptions have been lost or oversimplified, and confirm it is current before publishing. AI helped him draft; verifying and approving the wording is his professional responsibility.

Leadership & Management

Managers and leaders may use AI to:

  • summarise strategic documents
  • compare policy options
  • prepare briefing papers
  • analyse consultation responses
  • identify emerging themes
  • support planning and decision-making

AI can inform decisions but should never replace leadership, experience or professional responsibility.

Accessibility & Inclusion

AI has enormous potential to improve accessibility. Examples include:

  • creating alternative text for images
  • generating captions and transcripts
  • simplifying complex language
  • translating content
  • producing materials in different formats

These outputs should always be reviewed to ensure they are accurate, appropriate and meet the needs of the intended audience.

Key messageA common thread

Although these examples span different roles, they share one thing: AI is used to support people, not replace them. Across every role:

  • people ask the questions
  • people provide the context
  • people evaluate the outputs
  • people make the decisions
  • people remain accountable.

Technology may change; professional judgement remains constant.
